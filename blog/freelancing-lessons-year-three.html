Three Years Freelancing:
Lessons I Wish I Had Earlier

BJ Chiawa
Designer & Developer
April 29, 2026 9 min read

Three years ago, I left a stable engineering job, told myself I'd freelance for six months to see how it felt, and never went back. The six months became a year, then two, then three. I've worked with startups that ran out of money, agencies that paid slowly, founders who changed their minds weekly, and a handful of clients I'd drop everything for again.

Nobody gave me a manual. I made expensive mistakes early and cheap ones later, which is roughly the right order. This post is the manual I would have wanted - not inspirational, just accurate.

Lesson 1: You are almost certainly undercharging

1

When I started, I based my rate on what I'd been earning as a salaried employee, divided it by working hours, and added a small buffer for uncertainty. This is the wrong calculation. It ignores taxes, the weeks with no work, the time spent on proposals and admin, equipment, software, professional development, health insurance if you're buying your own, and the fact that a freelancer carries all the business risk that an employer used to absorb.

A more honest starting point: take your target annual income, double it, then divide by the number of billable hours you can realistically sell in a year. If you work 48 weeks and bill 25 hours per week — leaving room for admin, sales, and the odd slow month - that's 1,200 hours. A target income of $80,000 becomes a rate of around $133/hour before you've accounted for anything. Most people I know started at half this and spent a year slowly adjusting upward.

Raising your rate also changes the type of client you attract. Clients who push hardest on price are usually the ones who will push hardest on everything else. A higher rate filters for clients who are buying the outcome, not hunting for the lowest number.

The best clients I've had never once asked me to justify my rate. They asked what I needed to do the work well.

Lesson 2: Bad clients are a choice you make

2

Early on, I took every project I could get. Some were fine. A few were genuinely good. And two were bad enough that I would have been better off financially doing nothing - once you account for the stress, the scope creep, the late payments, and the opportunity cost of the better work I couldn't take because I was busy.

There are signals. They were there in both cases. I ignored them because I needed the money and told myself I was being too picky.

The signals that have never been wrong for me:

  • They are in a rush to start, but unclear on what they actually want.
  • They want to see a lot of work done before signing anything.
  • They talk about previous freelancers in a way that makes every problem the freelancer's fault.
  • They ask for your "best rate" before they've told you the scope.
  • They are slow to respond during the sales process, then expect immediate turnaround once contracted.
  • They describe the project as "simple" or "quick" when it is neither.

None of these is a guarantee of a bad engagement, but when two or more are present, I now walk away. The work I've turned down based on these signals has never turned out to be work I wish I'd taken.

Lesson 3: The contract protects the relationship, not just you

3

I used to find contracts awkward to bring up with clients I liked. It felt like I was implying I didn't trust them. A designer I worked with early in my freelance career reframed this for me: a contract is what lets two people who trust each other work together without misunderstanding. It's not a sign of distrust — it's what makes trust possible.

The things a contract must cover, without exception:

  • Scope — what is included, and what is explicitly not included.
  • Payment terms — amount, schedule, and what happens if payment is late.
  • Revision rounds — how many are included before additional charges apply.
  • Who owns the work — and when that ownership transfers (usually upon full payment).
  • Kill fee — what you receive if the project is cancelled after work has begun.
  • How disputes are resolved, and which jurisdiction's law applies.

The kill fee is the one most new freelancers leave out. I left it out once. A client cancelled a six-week engagement at week four after I'd turned down other work to take it. I had no contractual right to anything beyond the invoices I'd already sent. I now include a kill fee of 25–50% of the remaining project value, depending on the project size. No client with good intentions has ever objected to it.

Practical note: you don't need a lawyer to have a solid contract. The Contract Killer (open source, written by Andy Clarke) is a good starting point — plain English, comprehensive, and widely respected. Adapt it to your situation and have a lawyer review it once if you can afford it.

Lesson 4: Scope creep is a communication failure, not a client failure

4

Every freelancer complains about scope creep. Clients ask for "one small thing" that turns into three weeks of unpaid work. I complained about it for the first year. Then I noticed that the clients where scope crept the most were the clients where I'd been least specific upfront.

When a client asks for something outside the agreed scope, the right response is not frustration — it's a change order. A short document or email that states what the new work is, how long it will take, and what it will cost. Clients who genuinely understand what they're asking for will accept this. Clients who thought it was "just a small tweak" will recalibrate their expectations.

The muscle you're building is the ability to say, calmly and without apology: "That's outside what we agreed — here's what it would take to add it." This is not confrontational. It's professional. Most clients respect it. The ones who don't are telling you something useful about how the rest of the engagement will go.

Lesson 5: The habit that changed everything

5

Everything above is about the business mechanics of freelancing. This one is about the work itself, and it made a bigger difference than any of the others.

I started sending a short written summary at the end of every week to every active client. Not a timesheet. Not a status update in a project management tool. A plain-text email, usually five to eight sentences, that answered three questions: what did I complete this week, what am I working on next week, and is there anything I need from you to keep moving.

The effect was immediate. Clients stopped chasing me for updates — because they already had them. Trust went up because there was a consistent record of progress. Scope discussions became easier because there was a shared understanding of what had been done. And my own work improved because writing the summary forced me to assess, honestly, whether the week had moved the project forward or just felt busy.

Several clients have told me this alone was what made them refer me to others. It takes fifteen minutes and costs nothing.

Three years out, what actually matters

Freelancing is not passive income, and it's not freedom in the Instagram sense. It's running a small business where you are also the product. That has real advantages — autonomy, variety, no performance review season, no office politics — and real costs, mainly that the business never fully switches off.

The people I know who've made it work long-term have a few things in common. They are specific about what they do — not "I do web stuff" but "I build data dashboards for logistics companies." They charge enough that slow months don't become crises. They have two or three clients they return to repeatedly, not thirty one-off projects a year. And they treat their own time with the same respect they'd demand a client show for it.

None of this is complicated. Most of it I knew in theory before I started. The gap between knowing and doing it was about eighteen months of making the predictable mistakes. Hopefully, reading this shortens that gap for someone.

If you're thinking about making the jump, or you're six months in and wondering whether it gets easier: it does, once you stop treating every awkward conversation as something to avoid. The awkward conversations — about rate, scope, contracts, and expectations - are the job. Get comfortable with them, and the rest follows.
