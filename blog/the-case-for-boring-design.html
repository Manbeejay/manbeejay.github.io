The Case for Boring Design

BJ Chiawa
Designer & Developer
April 12, 2025 6 min read

Every few months, a new design trend arrives, and everyone rushes to implement it. Glassmorphism, neumorphism, bento grids, dark mode, everything. Designers retweet it, Dribbble fills up with explorations, and product teams start asking whether their app looks "up to date."

Meanwhile, the products people use most often - banking apps, productivity tools, email clients, e-commerce checkouts - mostly look the same as they did five years ago. And they work better for it.

Boring works because it disappears

The job of an interface is to get out of the user's way. A person who opens your app to check their account balance doesn't want to admire the card shadows. They want their balance. The best design in that context is the one the user doesn't notice at all.

"Boring" in this sense means predictable. Users have built up mental models of how software works over years of use. Clear labels. Consistent button placement. Forms that behave like forms. When you break from these patterns in search of originality, you're not being creative - you're creating friction and calling it personality.

The most useful interface is often the one no one ever describes as beautiful.

The novelty trap

There's a particular kind of design that gets a lot of attention and very few satisfied users. It screenshots well. It wins awards. Investors use it in pitch decks to show how "polished" a startup looks. But when real people sit down and try to complete a task, it fails.

The problem isn't bad intentions. Designers want to make something they're proud of. But when the audience for your work is other designers on Dribbble, you optimize for different things than when the audience is someone trying to renew their car insurance on a phone with a cracked screen.

What boring actually means in practice

Here's what I mean when I say "boring design" is worth chasing:

  • Use standard components for standard actions. A close button should look like a close button.
  • Write labels that say what the button actually does. "Submit" rarely says enough. "Save changes" does.
  • Don't invent new interaction patterns when an existing one works. Tabs are tabs. Dropdowns are dropdowns.
  • Keep the visual hierarchy simple enough that users don't need to think about it.
  • Test with real users who aren't designers. Watch where they get stuck.

None of that sounds exciting. It isn't exciting. It's just what works, and working is what we're paid for.

When originality is the point

None of this means design should be homogenous. Brand expression, marketing pages, editorial design, portfolio work - these contexts reward originality. The user's goal there is to experience the work, not to complete a task. When the design itself is the product, being forgettable is the failure mode.

The discipline is knowing which situation you're in. Building a checkout flow? Go boring. Designing the homepage that gets people to that checkout? You have room to make a statement.

The next time someone on your team says the UI feels "a bit safe," ask what problem that's actually solving. If the answer involves users and tasks, keep it safe. If it involves differentiation and brand, then talk. Those are different conversations that deserve different answers.
