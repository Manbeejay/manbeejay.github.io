Overview

In 2012, a group of pilgrims from Enugu made the journey to the Holy Land. The flight went out of Port Harcourt, with a three-hour stopover at Antalya Airport in Turkey, before landing at Ben Gurion International Airport in Tel Aviv.

Israel is layered in a way that no other place quite is. Every stone carries a reference, every hill a name, every road a story that has been told for two thousand years. Walking those sites as a person of faith is a different experience from reading about them. The ground makes the text real.

The sites visited span three regions: Jerusalem and the Old City, the Galilee in the north, and several additional sacred locations spread across the country.

Regions

Jerusalem

Jerusalem holds the core of Christian pilgrimage. The Old City packs more history into its ancient walls than most countries carry in their entirety. To walk it as a believer is to move through a living text.

Church of the Holy Sepulchre

Built over both Golgotha — the hill of the crucifixion — and the tomb of the resurrection, this is the most venerated site in Christianity. The stone of anointing, the dark interior of the rotunda, the queue of pilgrims waiting to enter the aedicule: none of it prepares you for actually being there.

The Western Wall (Wailing Wall)

The last remaining structure of the Second Temple, and the holiest site in Judaism. Thousands of notes folded into its ancient stones, prayers left in the cracks. The plaza in front of it is vast and quiet in a way that a busy place rarely is.

Via Dolorosa

The path Jesus walked to his crucifixion, marked by the traditional 14 Stations of the Cross. The streets of the Old City narrow and twist as the route winds through them. Pilgrims carry wooden crosses on Fridays. The weight of the symbolism is felt at every turn.

Mount of Olives & Garden of Gethsemane

The Mount of Olives looks directly across the Kidron Valley at the Old City walls. Gethsemane sits at its foot, with olive trees that are centuries old. Dominus Flevit — the teardrop-shaped church where Jesus wept over Jerusalem — offers one of the most moving views in the city. The Church of All Nations stands beside the rock where Jesus prayed before his arrest.

The Garden Tomb

Outside the Old City walls, the Garden Tomb offers a serene alternative site for the burial and resurrection. Favoured by many Protestant pilgrims, the garden itself is quiet and carefully kept — a space for reflection that the crowded Holy Sepulchre cannot always provide.

The Galilee Region

Northern Israel holds most of the sites of Jesus' active ministry. The Sea of Galilee is smaller than the imagination builds it, which makes the stories feel closer, not further. The hills are green, the water is still in the mornings, and the place has a quietness that Jerusalem, for all its holiness, does not have.

Nazareth

The boyhood home of Jesus. The Basilica of the Annunciation is built directly over the grotto where the angel Gabriel appeared to Mary. St. Joseph's Church stands over the traditional site of Joseph's carpentry workshop. The old city still carries traces of how Nazareth looked in the first century.

Basilica of the Annunciation St. Joseph's Church Boyhood of Jesus

Capernaum

On the northern shore of the Sea of Galilee, Capernaum was Jesus' adopted base of ministry. The ruins of a first-century synagogue stand here, and an octagonal church has been built over the site of Peter's house. It is possible to stand where the text says he stood.

Mount of Beatitudes

A gentle hill above the Sea of Galilee, where Jesus gave the Sermon on the Mount. The Church of the Beatitudes sits at the top, surrounded by gardens. The view across the water from here is one of the most peaceful in Israel. The Beatitudes on the church windows read differently when you are standing on the hill where they were first spoken.

Tabgha

The Church of the Multiplication of the Loaves and Fishes stands at Tabgha, on the shore of the Sea of Galilee. A famous ancient mosaic inside depicts the loaves and fishes. Simple, small, and extraordinarily precise in its witness to a miracle.

Jordan River — Yardenit

Yardenit is the traditional baptismal site just south of the Sea of Galilee, where the river flows out of the lake. Pilgrims in white robes wade into the water to be baptised or to renew their baptism. The river is narrower and greener than most people expect. The moment carries weight regardless of what you bring to it.

Additional Sacred Sites

Beyond Jerusalem and Galilee, several other sites mark events that anchor the Christian story. Each one demands its own stop.

Mount Tabor

Rising steeply from the Jezreel Valley, Mount Tabor is the traditional site of the Transfiguration, where Jesus appeared in radiant glory before Peter, James, and John. The Basilica of the Transfiguration crowns the summit. The drive up the narrow switchback road is an experience in itself.

Transfiguration Basilica of the Transfiguration Jezreel Valley

Cana (Kafr Kanna)

Cana is where Jesus performed his first recorded miracle, turning water into wine at a wedding feast. The Wedding Church here marks the site. Many pilgrims bring their wedding rings to be blessed. The town is small and the church modest, but the significance sits larger than the surroundings.

Bethlehem

A short distance south of Jerusalem in the West Bank, Bethlehem is home to the Church of the Nativity, built over the grotto traditionally identified as the birthplace of Jesus. Entering through the Door of Humility, a deliberately low entrance that forces every visitor to bow, is the first thing that happens. What comes next is harder to put into words.
